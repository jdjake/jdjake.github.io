The Basic Game Procedure

  1. The Referee Describes the situation, depicting the environment, characters, hazards, and objects present, and noting any smells, sounds, or other features.
  2. The players ask Questions to clarify description elements.
  3. The players put themselves in their characters shoes, discussing what to do, and then Declaring Actions they wish to perform. This can be anything imaginable, from speaking to characters present, interacting with the environment, or using a special capability granted to them by their Kindred or Class. The Referee clarifies potential risk involved with such actions. Each Character may perform a different action, though it usually beneficial for a Party to stick together and act in concert with one another.
  4. The Referee Judges the potential results of the proposed action. Specific game rules may apply, and an appropriate Resolution System may apply, or the Referee will come up with a ruling based on the specific situation. The Referee presents the odds to a player, making the likelihood of success and failure and potential risks clear. The player may then alter their approach, or come up with alternate plans to tip the odds in their favor.
  5. The action is Resolved, potentially involving some kind of dice roll. Then Time Passes, depending on the action taken. Time is normally divided into discrete segments called Rounds (10 Seconds) and Turns (10 minutes). In hazardous environments, a Wandering Monster roll may be made to determine if the Party encounters something by chance.

Resolution Systems

Skill Checks

Some actions require passing a Skill Check to succeed. All adventurers can use the Listen, Search, and Survival skills. Some classes grant the use of additional, specialized skills. To make a Skill Check, roll a d6, and add any situational modifiers. If the roll equals or exceeds the character’s Skill Target, they succeed in the Skill Check, and the action they carry out succeeds. A natural 1 always fails and a natural 6 always succeeds.

Ability Checks

An Ability Check represents a test of a character’s physical or mental aptitudes (one of their Ability Scores) rather than a particular learned skill. Roll a d6 and add the Ability Modifier of the relevant Ability. If the result equals or exceeds a target number of 4, the check succeeds. A natural 1 always fails and a natural 6 always succeeds.

Saving Throws

Certain dangerous or detrimental effects call for a Saving Throw, or Save, which is a roll to determine whether a character avoids the effect. There are 5 Save Categories, each for a different type of hazard. Roll a d20 and add applicable modifiers. If the result equals or exceeds the character’s Save Target for the relevant Save Category, the Saving Throw Succeeds.

Doom are effects that instantly kill or inflict ongoing physical malignancies, such as poison, disease, and death magic. Ray are effects coming from rapidly moving, directional effects, such as lightning bolts. Hold are effects that hinder or prevent movement, such as paralysis or falling rocks that may crush or pin characters. Blast are effects that fill a wide area, such as explosions. Spell applies to potent, directly targeted effects, such as arcane or holy spells.

In situation not covered by the rules, the Referee should run down the categories listed above, choosing the first that fits the effect in question. If an effect does not fit in any category, default to Doom.

Attack Rolls

This roll is used to determine whether an attacker lands a blow on an opponent. Roll a d20 and add any applicable modifiers (your Attack bonus, as well as your Strength Modifier for Melee Attacks, and Dexterity Modifier for Ranged Attacks). If you beat or exceed the defender’s Armor Class, you succeed.

Luck Check

Used when the outcome of a situation depends on pure chance. The Referee proposes a chance of success, and a relevant dice is rolled to determine if the Luck Check succeeds.

Example Resolutions

  • If players wish to make a reasonable climb (e.g. climbing a tree) in a non-pressured situation, no Check may be required. In more difficult or tense situations, a Dexterity Ability Check may be required to succeed, with a Failure resulting in the Character falling halfway through making the climb.

  • A stuck door that is unlocked but so obstructed that it cannot be opened without force may be shoulder barged with a successful Strength Ability Check. The Referee should probably warn the player if this will produce a loud noise, which may attract attention to the Party. Similarly, a Locked door may be broken open with an axe or crowbar with a successful Strength Ability Check. Alternatively a Locked Door may be picked by a thief using a Pick Lock Skill Check, or open via a magic spell (likely with no Check required).

  • When two groups approach one another, there is a chance one or the other will be unaware of the other’s presence. This is handled with a Surprise Roll, which is a Luck Check for each group, with odds depending on the vigilence of the other party.

Encounter Procedures

Encounter Procedures apply when adventurers meet strangers, foes, or monsters in the wild.

  1. The Referee determines the Awareness of each party of one another. This applies if a group is making a lot of noise, or carrying a light in the dark.
  2. The Referee rolls for Surprise, if one party is not aware of the other. The standard chance of being surprised is 2-6, but may be modified in certain situations.
  3. The Referee determines the Distance between the two groups when encountered.
  4. The Referee determines the Initiative, i.e. which side acts first. Each side rolls a d6, the highest roll determining whom goes first.
  5. Both Sides declare their intended Actions. If one side decides to start attacking the other side, begin following Combat Procedures. If one side wishes to avoid the encounter, it may attempt to Flee the encounter. A side may attempt to Parley with the other, and provided combat has not been initiated, a Charisma bonus may determine a Monster or NPC Reaction to the party.

Combat Procedures

  1. Both sides declare their Intended Actions. The intention to cast a spell must be declared, because the spell is disrupted if the caster loses initiative and is harmed. The intention to flee from melee must be declared, for opponents that win initiative against a fleeing combatant are able to attack their opponents with a bonus.
  2. Each side rolls d6 to determine Initiative, who acts first.
  3. Each side, in order, moves and attacks. Combatants may not move while casting spells.
  4. The Referee makes a Morale Check for NPCs, if applicable.

House Rules

Death and Dismemberment

HP are an abstract measurement representing your character’s ability not to get hit, or at least not to get hit seriously. A PC reduced to 0 HP can still function normally. Any damage dealt in excess of HP is Lethal Damage. Players dealt Lethal Damage make a Death and Dismemberment Roll. Lethal Damage normally kills non-player characters, but large, vicious, or tenacious creatures may make Death and Dismemberment Rolls as well.

To make a Death and Dismemberment, roll 1d12, then add the amount of Lethal Damage your character has taken, and your character’s current number of injuries, to determine a number X. Consult the following table, using the column that best represents the injury your character has taken. If it is not narratively clear, roll 1d6 to determine the location of a lethal injury.

                 
Roll 1 Arm 2 Leg 3-4 Torso 5-6 Head Acid / Fire Cold / Frost Lightning Magic
1+ Disabled X Days Disabled X Days Rib Split X Days Concussed X Days Burned X Days Frostbite X Days Burned X Days Anathema X Days
11+ Mangled Mangled Crushed Skullcracked Blind Frozen Deaf Marked
16+ X-15 Fatal Wounds X-15 Fatal Wounds X-15 Fatal Wounds X-15 Fatal Wounds X-15 Fatal Wounds X-15 Fatal Wounds X-15 Fatal Wounds X-15 Fatal Wounds

If a PC recieves a Fatal Wound, they have 3 rounds to clear all their Fatal Wounds, or they die. PCs with Fatal Wounds are unconscious. This does not mean they are quiet or asleep (e.g. they can be screaming their head off), but it means they cannot take conscious actions. No hopping away, nothing helpful, useful, or sensible.

A player can attempt to remove a Fatal Wound at the end of their turn by rolling a 1 on a d6. An adjacent ally can attempt to remove a Fatal Wound by spending their turn doctoring the wounded PC and then rolling under half their Intelligence (potentially boosted with narratively appropriate equipment). Up to two allies can help at once, but no more (although they might lend bonuses to the roll), and only two Fatal Wounds can be removed per round by doctoring, no matter how many allies are present.

Magical healing removes 1 Fatal Wound per 2 points of HP it would normally restore.

The goal of this table is to make characters less fragile, while still heavily penalizing getting injured. If your HP is zero, and you could retreat, and you didn’t, then the consequences are on your head. They also make retirement more tmepting. If your PC survives to reach Level 5, they can retire to safety. They get to escape the Death and Dismemberment Table, the Early Retirement Table, and the whims of fate forever. That’s the risk-reward balance. Risk injury to get money. Get money to avoid risking injury, possibly forever. Or risk it some more to possibly, maybe, become a legend.

  • Anathema: Cannot benefit from magical healing or helpful magic.
  • Blind: Unable to see. Disadvantage to attack rolls and Advantage to those attacking you.
  • Burned: Cannot wear armor (it is excruciatingly painful). Afterwards, gain an Interesting Scar.
  • Crushed: Make Save. If they fail, roll on the table below. If they pass, they gain an Interesting Scar: (1) Permanently lose 1 Strength (2) Permanently lose 1 Dexterity (3) Permanently lose 1 Constitution (4) Crushed Throat. Cannot speak louder than a whisper (5) Crushed Guts. Constitution is 4 for the purposes of holding your breath. Can swim; can’t dive. (6) Broken Spine. Paralyzed from the neck down. Save to recover after 1d6 days. If failed, Save again after 1d6 weeks. If both Saves are failed, the PC is permanently paralyzed.
  • Concussed: TODO.
  • Deaf: Cannot hear.
  • Disabled A injured limb becomes unusable. A Disabled arm cannot hold anything. A Disabled leg cannot support the PC’s weight; they fall prone, and their movement speed is halved. A PC with two Disabled legs but two working arms can crawl at 1/20th their normal movement speed.
  • Frostbite: The PC’s Dexterity becomes 4 for the purposes of delicate tasks (card tricks, lockpicking, tying shoelaces) Fingertips, ears, and nose go black and may fall off.
  • Frozen: Save. If passed, lose d6 digits of fingers or feet (randomly chosen using d20). Lost digits are still attached, but will snap off or melt away. If failed, roll d6: (1) Lose 2d6 digits (2) lose all but one digit (3) lose both hands (4) lose both feet (5) lose both hands, feet, and nose (6) Lose both hands, feet, nose, and eyes (become Blind).
  • Mangled: Save. If failed, lose a Limb. It is permanently Disabled. If pass, Save again. On a success, gain an Interesting Scar. If fail, lose a digit.
  • Marked: Save. If Failed, any magical attack against the PC does double damage, applies a -4 penalty to Save, or has a suitably nasty enhanced effect, like cursing their bloodline or stealing all their memories. If they pass, any magical attack against the PC automatically deals 1 extra damage or has a minor extra effect, like setting their hair on fire or cursing their dog. They also gain an Interesting Scar, usually known as a Curse Mark.
  • Rib Split: The PC’s maximum HP is reduced by the PC’s level.
  • Skullcracked: Save. If Failed, roll on the table below. If they pass, they gain an Interesting Scar (1) Permanently lose 1 Intelligence. (2) Permanently lose 1 Wisdom (3) Permanently lose 1 Charisma (4) Left eye lost. -1 to ranged attacks. If both eyes lost, Blind. (5) Right eye lost. -1 to ranged attacked. If both eyes lost, Blind. (6) Coma. Save to recover after 1d6 days. If failed, Save again after 1d6 weeks. If both Saves are failed, the PC is permanently unconscious.

An Interesting Scar is a mangled, gnarled thing, a worm of flesh buried under the skin. A gouge. Flesh warped and bent, like paint scraped before it fully dried. The player must describe their PC’s Interesting Scar. It provides a +2 bonus to Charisma when interacting with people who appreciate scars (barbarians, deep-cave salamanders, suitors attracted to danger, poets), but provides a -2 bonus to Charisma where narratively important. They can also be used to identify you.

Scars can trump other scars. Generally, if comparing scars, it’s wise to start with the least interesting and work your way up. If anyone has a else more interesting scar (as judged by the table or a roll-off), they get the +2 bonus. Use the HD of the creature that inflicted the scar as a guide for which scars are more impressive. Alternatively, lie.

Early Retirement Table

At level 5, you can retire your PC to safety. This means I won’t torment them any more. If they can afford it, they can buy a or rent some land, set up a shop, teach at a wizard college, or beg in the gutter. They won’t affect the plot anymore, but the plot won’t specifically affect them. General disasters (fire, plagues, war, demonic invasions) will still affect their lives, but they are safe from almost anything else. Feel free to organize your character’s retirement ahead of time. You can try and buy a castle, a tavern, or a political position.

If a player does not want to play a PC any more, but they haven’t reached Level 5 yet, create a Early Retirement Table for that PC. The character is no longer playable, but still exists in the world. Whenever it feels relevant, roll on the Early Retirement Table, and interpret the result to see what that character has gotten up to.

Example: Farmer

Tito the Toadling lost a leg and a toe. He wasn’t dead, exactly, but an Assassin with a missing leg and a strong distrust of the rest of the party isn’t fun to play any more. Tito retired early. They can still work the field, barely. So they swear fealty to a House Mulbreck, and are given a plot of land, hut and a long list of demands. Here’s Tito’s Early Retirement Table:

   
Roll Result
1 Prosperity. Extra food, good weather, or good luck.
2 Rumour. May have 1 interesting rumour for the PCs.
3-7 Stability. Just on the edge of starvation.
8 Inconvenience. A leak in the roof, a rat in the grain, a cracked tooth.
9 Demands. Taxed to the point of madness, worked to the point of collapse. No actual effect other than stress and frustration.
10 Dire Peril. Starvation looms.

Example: Monk

Almost anyone can leave the secular life and become a Monk. It can be a hard life, full of labour, prayers, and deprivation. Your crimes are forgiven. Only the most prosperous order would accept a completely crippled initiate, but the ability to read and write is always valued. In disordered times, monks and nuns might be no better than idle and debauched laymen. At the very least, a veneer of religious devotion is required.

   
Roll Result
1 Tranquility. The PCs hear a distant rumour of their former companion. They are doing well.
3-9 Isolation. No word of the ex-PC one way or the other.
10 Danger. The PCs hear a distant rumour that their friend’s order is threatened by war, plague, famine, or disorder.

Example: Criminal

No one can live outside the law forever. This option is open to anyone, but it’s a risk.

   
Roll Result
1 Escape. Stole enough to start again. The ex-PC vanishes. One day, in a distant land, they might see their old companions and nod slyly.
2-6 Edge of Starvation. No change, but the outlook is bleak.
7-8 Caught. Imprisoned and accused. Might be released, might be tortured, might be forgotten.
9-10 Executed. Or exiled, which is more or less the same thing when your money and goods have been confiscated.

Example: Beggar

Should an ex-PC be so cursed, mangled, mentally damaged, or dangerous as to make any other option unsuitable, they might be forced to live on the streets of a town or city. If the PCs send their friend money, or otherwise see to their well-being, the GM can reroll on the table and pick a better result.

   
Roll Result
1 Head Above Water. Food, a warm corner, a position in the local hierarchy of beggars, the favour of the local Church. Might even lead to some minor position out of the rain.
2 Minor Improvement. New pants, a hat, a few more coins than last week.
3-6 Edge of Starvation. No change.
7 Lost. No one has heard from the ex-PC for some time. They might emerge later, or they might be forgotten forever.
8 New Injury. Cold, disease, knife-fighting, trampled by a horse, kicked by a knight, poked by an urchin.
9 Crime. Accused, tried, locked in the stocks, beaten, robbed, cast out again.
10 Death. By disease, an accident, in a brawl, or by their accumulated injuries.

Camping Minigame

When ending a travel day in the wilderness, the Party must set up camp for the night. Each member of the party may take one of the following Setup activities. For each travel point spent, each member may take an additional activity.

Setup Activities

Preparing the Campsite

At least one PC must spend time clearing the ground, removing brush and small rocks, then setting up tents and bedrolls.

Search for Natural Shelter

If not carrying tents, a PC may spend time attempting to search for a natural place in which to take shelter, i.e. a small cave, a thick grove of trees, a tangled mass of vegetation. Weather that impairs visibility gives a disadvantage to this roll.

   
Biome Chance
Hills 3/6
Forest 2/6
Plains / Meadow 1/6
Swamp / Bog 0/6

Success grants the PCs Natural Shelter for the purpose of getting a good night’s sleep.

Care for Animals

Parties with hounds, mounts, or pack animals require at least one PC devoted to feeeding them, removing saddles and gear, grooming them, and caring for them before settling in for the night. This task also secures vehicles.

Starting a Fire

At least one PC must spend time building a fire circle, preparing the tinder, and protecting the new flame as it grows in strength. If there are strong winds, another PC is required to assist in this task.

This task requires fire-making tools or magic. If those tools are absent, no fire will be produced regardless of how much wood is gathered.

Gathering Firewood

Multiple PCs can be assigned to firewood duty. Each rolls 1d6 to determine how much wood they gather. If the ground is damp, add a -1 modifier. For precipitation, add a -2 modifier. For heavy precipiation, add a -4 modifier. In open terrain, apply a -2 to each result.

Fellowship

If a fire is created and at least one firewood is gathered, an idle PC can be assigned to fellowship. They prepare a warm meal, entertain, and comfort the others. Each PC has a 4 in 6 chance to remove a level of exhaustion if there is a successful fellowship. Automatic success if beverages and pipeleaf are used.

Nighttime Encounters

Once the camp is set up, the GM rolls 1d6 to determine if a nighttime encounter occurs. On a result of 1:

  1. Roll for Encounter Distance. The Encountered creature appears 2d6 x 30’ from camp. This distance is halved if 0-4 firewood is gathered, and doubled if 12+ firewood is gathered. The GM informs the party that something is moving around in the dark.

  2. Roll for surprise. If 0-4 firewood is gathered, the Encountered creature gets -1 to their result. If 12+ firewood is gathered, both get +1 to their result. If a hound or mounts are present, add +1 to the party’s result.

  3. Roll for Encounter type. If 0-4 Firewood is gathered, roll for a No Fire Encounter. If 5+ Firewood is gathered, roll for a Fire Encounter

  4. Roll for a Reaction. If the encountered creature is not immediately hostile, a randomly chosen PC makes a reaction roll. They were the party member on watch at the time of the encounter.

A New Day

Tally up Travel Point bonuses and penalties:

   
Lack of Bedroll or Tent / Natural Shelter -1 Travel Points Today
0-4 Firewood Gathered Winter (-2), Autumn or Spring (-1), Summer (-0) Travel Points Today
12+ Firewood Gathered +1 Travel Points Today

Once tallied up, if there is a Travel Point penalty, everyone gains a level of Exhaustion. If there is no Travel Point penalty, everyone heals 1HP. If there is a Travel Point bonus, everyone heals 2HP.

In addition, if a Nighttime Encounter occurred, Spell Preparation has a 1 in 6 chance of failure (per spell).

Alternate Alignment System

Law and Chaos are diametrical cosmic forces in the universe. The tension between Law and Chaos give rise to the very threads from which gods, fairy, mortals, and reality itself is woven. Law is frequently associated with ‘Goodness’, Chaos with ‘Evil’, but these are generalizations, not rules. Neither are truly benevolent or malevolent.

All beings have an Alignment which represents a character’s orientation on the cosmic scale, the ratios of the cosmic threads from which a person’s soul is woven. In practice, there are infinite ways by which a being’s soul may be tied to the universe, as many ways as there are styles to braid the cosmic threads together. But for the purposes of game mechanics, beings are categorized under one of three alignments: Law, Neutrality, or Chaos.

Alignments have nothing to do with a character’s allegiances, personality, or morality; whether or not a character with either of these alignments is actively engaged in the Cosmic Struggle, their souls are marked with a cosmic affiliation nonetheless. Animals and other creatures of lesser sentience tend to be neutral, but not always. Characters of Neutral Alignment are also embodiments of Law and Chaos, but these exist in a more balanced state within the character’s being than those of Lawful or Chaotic alignment. Neutrality is not a cosmic force in the universe.

Player characters are free to select whichever Alignment they choose, though Clerics must be Lawful, and Friars cannot be Chaotic. GM’s should not use Alignment as a tool to police the behavior of aligned characters. But there are powerful beings and institutions within The Dolmenwood with the ability to do so, as they entwine fates, drawing aligned characters to their banner’s cause. Because Alignments represent fundamental forces of the universe, a character’s Alignment may predicate very real mechanical effects and consequences as they interact with the magic, monsters, and more powerful beings within The Dolmenwood.

Law

Law is the force of order, stability, and harmony, but also stagnation, imprisonment, and fascism. The Cosmic Struggle of Law is to ensure the events of the world inevitably march on their flawless, unchanging plans towards the ultimate, irrefutable truth of the universe. As time moves on, all distraction and resistance to this plan will falter, until everything will be in its perfect state forevermore, without alteration or the possibility of possibilities. Those who are Lawful in Alignment are part of this inevitable destiny, awash on the turbulent seas of fate, but often have little knowledge of what that destiny is and what their role will be in fulfilling it. So those that pursue the Cosmic Struggle of Law are forever looking for signs and omens to show them their proper way.

Chaos

Chaos is the force of change and freedom, but also destruction, indifference, and lawlessness. Chaos is the howling maelstrom beyond the veil of shadows and existence. It bends and tears the fabric of the universe; it destroys all that seeks to be permanent. It allows great miracles as reality alters at the whim of those that can call upon the eldritch forces, and it causes great catastrophe as beings we call demons (and far, far worse) rip into our reality and lay waste to all. Everything that is made will be unmade. Nothing exists, and nothing can ever exist, not in a way that the cosmos can ever recognize. Those who pursue the Cosmic Struggle of Chaos consider the world in terms of ebbing and flowing energy, of eternal tides washing away the sand castles that great kings and mighty gods build for themselves. To be Chaotically Aligned is to be an attractor to higher beings and cosmic forces in need of pawns in the pursuit of needless goals. Many who are aligned with Chaos desperately wish they were not.

Neutrality

To be Neutral is merely to exist between the forces of Law and Chaos. Most beings in the universe are born with Neutral Alignment, and remain so unless drastic measures (often unwittingly followed) realign the cosmic threads of their existence.

In the real world, every human being that has ever existed has had Neutral Alignment; Law and Chaos are fantastical supernatural forces unlike any of us have experienced in our mundane lives. Examples of the most Lawfully Aligned characters are those deeply bound with the concept of fate, whether they are fulfilling a predetermined destiny or attempting to escape it. Such figures include Luke Skywalker, King Arthur, Paul Atreides, Buffy the Vampire Slayer, or The Antichrist. The most Chaotically Aligned characters are granted supernatural powers to change the world as they will it, or characters which always seem to run into destruction or misfortune. Examples of Chaotic characters include Superman, most wizards, and almost all of The Muppets.
