Guide for 5e GMs

Running Dolmenwood is much like running 5e, with the following main differences:

  • Exploration consists of Turns. Roll for a random encounter between each Turn. Actions take time, so players should act with purpose to avoid having too many random encounters. But not every encounter is bad.
  • Use Reaction Rolls and Surprise Rolls when encounters happen to make combat more dynamic. Most monsters (aside from programmed robots and mindless skeletons) prefer to avoid a fight unless the reward clearly outweighs the deadly risk.
  • You rarely need to set target numbers. Most targets are preset or on the character sheet. Add modifiers if you want, but I almost never do.
  • No Perception Checks. If players might be interested of something visible nearby, give a brief description without a roll, and provide more detail if players ask about it (players choosing to listen acts as a perception check). Players may choose to use their Search Skill to find truly hidden things, which is kind of like a perception check, but this can take serious time.
  • Spells are mostly like D&D. Arcane spells must be memorized before casting. Holy Spells are similar, but an hour of prayer to the saints associated with each spell is required each sunrise to refresh spells. Fairy magic refreshes each moonrise without a required ritual. There are no cantrips.
  • No Short Rests. Recovery takes time. PCs get 1HP back for having a good nights sleep, and 2HP while recovering in town.
  • Players may have Retainers, PC-like characters who join the party in exchange for a share of the treasure. Players mostly direct the actions of retainers assigned to them. But they should be treated as fellow party members. If the party gets a retainer to take risks above or beyond what the other PCs are doing, feel free to take charge of their roleplay: they might demand something in return, such as first dibs on treasure or an item a PC is carrying, or make a loyalty check if forced.

Time

Divided into Turns (10 minutes) and Rounds (10 seconds). Rounds are almost always for combat, and Turns almost always for exploration. In a combat round, players get to move and do essentially one thing (no quick actions). The same is true in Turns, though on a larger scale (moving to an adjacent turn, performing an action that takes a longer amount of time).

Rolls

Ability Checks

Players make a d6 roll rather than a d20 roll, adding their ability modifier. Success on a 4+.

Saving Throws

Players roll d20 and get equal to or above a target on their character sheet. There are five categories of saves:

  • Doom Saves for death, poison, disease, and curses.
  • Ray Saves for fast, directional effects (e.g. lightning).
  • Hold Saves for effects that restrain or paralyze.
  • Blast Saves for explosive effects.
  • Spell saves for potent, directly targeted effects.

Attack Rolls + Damage Rolls

Exactly like D&D.

Death Rolls

Monsters die when they get to 0HP. PCs use a Death and Dismemberment table when they hit 0HP. Players roll a d12, with higher rolls indicating worse injuries (11+ injuries are permanent). Full explanation is on the site. but players can help you with this when it happens.

Skill Checks

Player declares the skill they want to carry out. All PCs have Listen, Search, and Survival Skills. Other Classes have specialized skills (Detect Magic, Monster Lore, Stealth, etc). Players will explain what these are to you.

There’s two kinds of Skill Checks (with Stealth Skill Checks being a weird exception):

  • Secret Rolls: You Roll, for actions that can be repeated by players (but each check requires a Turn).
  • Public Rolls: Players Roll, for actions that cannot be repeated.

Players will tell you whether the Skill Check is Secret or Public.

For Secret Rolls, a player who spends an hour (6 Turns) succeeds automatically. Otherwise, the player spends a Turn to carry out the Skill Check. Roll d6, trying to get equal to or above a target on the PC’s character sheet. It is usually a 6, but can be lower for specialist skills. Don’t tell the player whether they succeeded or failed, though on a success give relevant information if it exists.

When searching an environment during a Turn, if players explicitly declare how they’re searching in a way that should reveal something, just tell them what they find. A Search Skill Roll is mainly used to abstractly search a room, at the cost of a serious chunk of time.

A Stealth Skill Check is made when an encounter happens, and the monsters encountered are not surprised by the PCs. Those with a Stealth Skill can make an additional Stealth Skill roll to see if they stay hidden.

Loyalty Checks

If the party forces a retainer to do something they’d rather not do, or if the loyalty of the retainer is tested, roll 2d6 secretly. If the roll is equal to or above the retainer’s Loyalty, the check succeeds and the retainer remains true to the party. If the check fails, the retainer begins making plans to abandon / betray / flee from the party.

Reaction Rolls

Used when figuring out how a monster will initially react to the party. Roll 2d6 (this is the roll I most often employ modifiers for, if the party seems more trustworthy, violent, etc to the monsters found). If the party approaches the monster non-violently for parley, also add the highest interacting PCs Charisma modifier:

Roll Reaction
2- Attacks immediately
3-5 Hostile, may attack
6-8 Uncertain, wary
9-11 Indifferent, may negotiate
12+ Eager, friendly

Treat the Reaction as inspiration to start roleplaying the encounter. Player actions will likely change the reaction (i.e. a monster will not remain Uncertain or Wary if the party draws their weapons, it’ll likely run for it, or call for friends and become hostile).

Combat

Unlike D&D, initiative is rolled each round, one roll for each group in the initiative (usually, one roll for PCs, and one roll for whomever they encounter). The order is as follows:

  1. Hint how the NPCs will act in the coming round.
  2. Players declare their actions for the round.
  3. Roll Initiative. A d6 for each side. On a tie, humans go first, with the groups remaining acting simultaneously.
  4. Each side acts. If a character is successfully hit before they cast a spell they declare in Step 2, they lose the ability to cast their spell. Characters must broadly follow the action declared before initiative, but can change things slightly (e.g. an NPC preparing to swing their sword at a foe who sees a PC beginning to cast a spell nearby may run over and try and hit them instead).
  5. Check Morale if something significant has happened which could effect morale. Roll 2d6 for the group (the highest morale of those in the group). If the roll is above the group’s morale, they try to either surrender to or flee from the PCs next round.
